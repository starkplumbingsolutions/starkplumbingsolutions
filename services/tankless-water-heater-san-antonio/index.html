How Tankless Water Heaters Work

A tankless unit heats water only when a hot tap or shower opens. A flow sensor triggers a gas burner or electric element, so you get continuous hot water and skip the standby heat loss of a storage tank. Performance depends on flow rate in gallons per minute (GPM) and the temperature rise your home needs, which is why sizing matters.

♨️

Tankless Water Heater Installation

Gas units need an adequately sized gas line, proper venting (direct vent or condensing, depending on the model) and a condensate drain for condensing models. Electric units draw heavy current. Many whole-home models need a dedicated high-amperage circuit, and some need more than one. Mounting location, clearances and a water-quality plan all shape the install.

💧

Tankless Water Heater Repairs

Error codes often point to a flow sensor, ignition or igniter, a scaled heat exchanger, a venting problem or a failed temperature sensor. Resetting the unit over and over without finding the cause can hide a serious fault. We read the code, test the parts and explain what it means before recommending a fix.

🔥

Sizing for Household Hot Water Demand

An undersized tankless unit gives lukewarm water when two showers run at once. An oversized one costs more than it needs to. We size by the number of fixtures used at the same time and the flow each one needs, then match the unit to your gas or electric supply.

🧪

Scale Buildup and Descaling

San Antonio water is hard, and tankless heat exchangers are sensitive to scale. Mineral buildup reduces flow and efficiency and can cause overheating. Manufacturers typically call for periodic descaling, and many warranties depend on proper water treatment. A whole-home softener or filtration system can protect the unit. We can assess your water first.

⚖️

Tank vs. Tankless: Which Fits Your Home?

A tank suits homes that want lower upfront cost and simple service. A tankless unit suits homes that want continuous hot water, have space for wall mounting and will keep up with maintenance. Either can be the right answer, and we will show you the difference using your own numbers.

🛠️

Gas or Electric Tankless in San Antonio Homes

Gas tankless units deliver high flow and suit homes with a properly sized gas line. Many older houses have a gas line sized for a tank heater, and a tankless burner needs much more gas at once, so the line often needs upsizing. Electric tankless units avoid gas and venting, but whole-home models draw heavy current, and some need more than one dedicated circuit. A panel with no spare capacity may need an upgrade before the unit can go in. Homeowners considering electric tankless for an all-electric house, often replacing an attic unit, should have a licensed electrician involved in the wiring.

One more local detail: neither type works during a power outage, because gas units still need electricity for the controls and fan. If outages worry you, ask about backup options.

📋

Hard Water, Softeners and Tankless Warranties

Neighbors and online forums say the same thing again and again: hard water is the biggest enemy of tankless heaters here. The heat exchanger has narrow passages, and scale narrows them further. Scale cuts flow, causes overheating and eventually clogs the exchanger. Many manufacturers write water-quality conditions into their warranties, and some expect treatment or regular descaling. Read your warranty before you buy, and keep service records so you can prove maintenance if you ever file a claim.

A properly sized whole-home softener protects the heater and the rest of the plumbing, including fixtures and appliances. We can test your water and show you the options before you decide.

🧰

What Tankless Error Codes Usually Point To

Every manufacturer uses its own code list, and the display number matters, so always write it down. In general, codes fall into a few families: ignition or flame failure, flow or water-sensor problems, overheating or high-limit trips, venting or exhaust blockage, and communication or board errors. A scaled heat exchanger can trigger several of them, which is why a code that keeps returning after a reset usually means a real cause.

Do not keep resetting a unit that shows a repeated ignition or venting code. A venting fault can allow exhaust gases into the home. We read the code, test the part and explain the result before we recommend a repair.

💡

What a Tankless Maintenance Visit Includes

A yearly visit is the cheapest way to protect the unit. We isolate the heater, flush the heat exchanger with a descaling solution through the service valves, clean the inlet filter, inspect the burner and ignition on gas units or the elements and wiring on electric units, check the venting and condensate drain, verify gas pressure, and test temperature and flow at the fixtures. Isolation valves make this possible, and if your system lacks them, we can add them during the first service.

🏠

Venting, Gas Lines and Why Retrofits Cost More

Swapping a tank for a tankless unit is not a like-for-like job. The venting is different, often a sealed plastic or stainless vent through the wall or roof, and condensing models need a drain for acidic condensate. Gas lines may need upsizing and permit inspection. In an attic, access, platform and clearances all matter. That is why retrofit quotes can look much higher than the price of the unit, and why local market ranges commonly run in the thousands.

🔧

Waiting for Hot Water and the Role of Recirculation

A common complaint is the wait. Tankless units heat on demand, but the hot water still has to travel through the pipe to your tap, and a long run to a far bathroom takes time. A short wait of twenty or thirty seconds is typical in a big house. If it is longer, a recirculation pump or a smaller-diameter line can shorten it. See our Hot Water Recirculation Pump service for that option.

Tankless units can also deliver a brief burst of cold water between uses, often called the cold water sandwich. Settings, recirculation and correct sizing reduce it.

🔍

Is Tankless Worth It? An Honest Look

Tankless units can save standby energy, free up floor space and provide continuous hot water, and many last longer than tanks when maintained. They also cost more up front, need regular descaling in hard-water areas and can be more complex to repair. For a small household with a simple layout, a tank may cost less overall. For a large family or a home with high hot water demand, tankless can be a good fit. We will walk through your actual numbers and tell you plainly which option we would choose in your house.

♨️

Sizing a Tankless Unit: A Worked Example

Suppose a family runs a shower at about 2 gallons per minute, a kitchen faucet at about 1.5, and a dishwasher or washer at about 2 at the same time. That is roughly 5.5 gallons per minute of demand. Next, the temperature rise: if incoming water is around 70 degrees in summer but much colder in winter, and you want 120 degrees at the tap, the unit must add 50 degrees or more. A unit that delivers that flow at that rise in winter is the one you size for, not the summer number. A model that falls short will give lukewarm water at peak use.

This is a simplified example, and real flow rates vary by fixture. We measure and size for your home, then match it to your gas or electric capacity.

💧

Tankless Safety: Venting, Carbon Monoxide and Clearances

Gas tankless units exhaust combustion gases, so venting must match the manufacturer's requirements for material, length, slope and termination. Clearances to windows, openings and combustibles matter. Install carbon monoxide alarms on each level and near sleeping areas, and test them regularly. If an alarm sounds, leave the home and call 911 or your gas utility. Never ignore soot around the unit, a yellow burner flame or repeated ignition error codes. Electric tankless units do not produce exhaust, but they need correct wire size and breaker rating to avoid overheating.

🔥

A Simple Tankless Maintenance Calendar

Keep the habit small. Each month, check the display for codes and look for drips or stains around the unit. Every few months, clean or inspect the inlet water filter if your model has one. Once a year, schedule a professional descale and inspection, and record the date. Every year, test your carbon monoxide alarms and look at the vent termination for debris, nests or damage. After any storm or power surge, check the unit for codes. If your water is hard, ask us whether a twice-a-year descale makes sense for your home. Records matter: they support your warranty if you ever need a claim.

If you move into a home with a tankless unit and no service history, treat it as overdue and schedule a descale and inspection in the first month.

🧪

When Tankless Repair Makes Sense and When Replacement Does

Many tankless problems are repairable: a scaled heat exchanger can be descaled, a flow or temperature sensor can be swapped, and an igniter or fan can be replaced. Repair usually makes sense on a unit that is under roughly ten years old and still has a sound heat exchanger. Replacement becomes the better choice when the heat exchanger is leaking or badly scaled, when parts are no longer available, or when repair costs approach the price of a new unit. We give you both numbers in writing and tell you which one we would choose in your home.

⚖️

Questions to Settle Before You Buy a Tankless Unit

Before you commit, confirm the gas line size or electrical capacity, the venting route, the water hardness, the location and the maintenance plan. Ask what the warranty requires and who handles claims. A good estimate answers these in writing, so that the unit you buy is the unit your home can actually support.

Seasonal Issues in San Antonio

  • Spring and summer: scale builds fastest with heavy use. Schedule descaling before peak months.
  • Fall: inspect venting for debris, nests and wear before cold weather.
  • Winter: demand spikes during cold showers and holidays. Do not ignore intermittent error codes.
  • After storms or outages: power surges can damage control boards. Have the unit checked after a major event.

Plain-English Glossary

GPM

Gallons per minute, the flow rate a unit can deliver.

Temperature rise

How many degrees the unit must add to incoming water.

Heat exchanger

The part that transfers heat to the water.

Condensing

A high-efficiency design that needs a condensate drain.

Isolation valves

Service valves that allow flushing.

Descaling

Removing mineral buildup with a cleaning solution.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a tankless system replace a traditional tank heater?

Yes, in many homes. The swap depends on gas line capacity, venting, electrical service and available space. We check those first so the unit is sized and installed properly.

Why is my tankless water heater showing an error code?

Codes usually indicate a sensor, ignition, flow, scale or venting issue. Avoid repeated resets. Call a technician to read the code and test the cause.

Does hard water affect tankless systems?

Yes. Scale on the heat exchanger reduces flow and efficiency and can cause overheating. Descaling on schedule and proper water treatment help protect the unit and its warranty.

How much does a tankless installation cost in San Antonio?

Published market ranges commonly run from about $5,000 to $7,000 depending on model, fuel and layout. Your written estimate covers your exact situation.

Do tankless water heaters work without electricity?

No. Gas models need electricity for controls and the fan, and electric models need it to heat. A power outage stops both unless you have backup power.

Do I need a water softener with a tankless heater?

It is strongly recommended in hard-water areas, and some warranties require water treatment or documented descaling. We can test your water and advise.

Why does my tankless heater make noise?

Rumbling often means scale in the heat exchanger. Clicking or whining can come from the igniter, fan or venting. A technician should diagnose it.

How often should a tankless water heater be flushed?

Many manufacturers recommend at least yearly, and hard-water homes may need more frequent descaling. Check your model's manual and ask us about your water.
