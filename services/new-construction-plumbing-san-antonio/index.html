Plumbing for New Residential Construction

Plumbing is the first trade on a new-build lot and the last to leave. Before concrete is poured, pipes are already in the ground. Before drywall goes up, supply, drain and vent lines are already in the walls. Mistakes made at either stage are expensive, because the fix usually means cutting into something that was just built.

That is why homeowners and builders in San Antonio ask for the same things: a plumber who shows up when the schedule says, passes inspection the first time, and can tell them exactly what is included. Those are also the complaints we see most in local builder and homeowner groups: no-shows that stall the framers, work that fails inspection, and quotes that grow once the job starts.

New construction plumbing means installing the entire plumbing system of a new structure: sewer connection, underground drains, water service, supply lines, drain-waste-vent (DWV) piping, gas piping where it applies, water heater, and every fixture. It differs from remodeling because nothing is hidden yet. Done right, you get clean runs, correct slopes and accessible shutoffs for the next thirty years.

What a complete new-build scope covers

  • Underground drain and waste lines from each fixture location to the sewer tie-in
  • Water service line from the meter to the house and main shutoff
  • Hot and cold supply piping to every fixture, appliance and hose bib
  • Drain, waste and vent piping through floors, walls and roof
  • Water heater, gas piping and shutoffs where specified
  • Fixture trim-out: toilets, sinks, faucets, tubs, showers, dishwasher and ice-maker connections
  • Pressure tests, camera or water tests as required, and inspection scheduling
Why it matters: Texas requires plumbing work to be done under a licensed plumber, and San Antonio requires permits and inspections at defined stages. We build those checkpoints into the schedule from day one.

Underground Plumbing Installation

Ground phase, also called underground rough-in, is the first plumbing visit and the one you cannot redo cheaply. We lay drain and waste piping in the trenches and under the slab area before the foundation is poured, set at the slope the code requires, and stub up every pipe in the exact spot the plans call for.

Why San Antonio soil changes the job

San Antonio sits on expansive clay in many areas and shallow limestone or caliche in others. Clay swells when wet and shrinks in drought, so slabs move. Rock makes trenching slower and harder on pipe bedding. Both affect how we protect pipe: proper bedding, sleeves where pipes pass through concrete, and no unsupported spans that can sag or crack as the ground shifts.

Foundation movement is the reason local homeowners warn each other about slab leaks. A pipe that is stressed, kinked or pinned against concrete at install can fail years later. Careful ground-phase work is the cheapest slab-leak prevention there is.

Ground-phase checklist we follow

  • Confirm fixture locations against the architectural and structural plans
  • Verify slope on every horizontal drain run
  • Sleeve or wrap pipes at slab penetrations
  • Install cleanouts where they stay accessible after finish flooring
  • Test the underground DWV system and schedule the city inspection before the slab is poured
  • Cap and protect all stub-ups so debris and concrete never enter the line
Don’t DIY this. Moving a stub-up after the pour means saw-cutting concrete. Confirm toilet, tub, shower and island sink locations with your builder before ground phase starts.

Water Supply and Drainage Systems

A new home needs three systems that work together: supply (clean water in), drain-waste (used water out) and vent (air so drains flow). Each has its own materials, rules and failure modes.

Water supply piping

We install supply lines sized for the number of fixtures and the expected flow. Material choice is a real decision, and it comes up constantly in homeowner forums. PEX is flexible, fast to install and resists freeze splitting better than rigid pipe. Copper is long-lived and well proven. CPVC is common in homes built in the 2000s, and buyers ask about it because of brittleness concerns on aging systems. We explain your options and what your builder's spec calls for, then install to that spec.

Drainage and venting

Drains rely on gravity, so slope and venting are everything. Too flat and solids settle. Too steep and water outruns solids. Poor venting causes slow drains, gurgling and sewer smell. We size and slope every run and keep vent routing clean through the attic and roof.

Plumbing Rough-In and the Build Timeline

Here is how plumbing moves through a typical new home, and where inspections fall. For a deeper dive, see our plumbing rough-in page.

  1. Pre-construction review

    We read the plans, count fixtures, confirm slab and wall locations, and give you a written scope and schedule.

  2. Ground phase

    Underground drain and waste piping is installed and tested before the foundation pour. City inspection happens before the slab.

  3. Top-out rough-in

    After framing, we run supply, DWV and vent piping through walls and floors, set tub and shower valves, and pressure-test.

  4. Rough-in inspection

    The city inspects the exposed rough plumbing. Insulation and drywall wait until it passes.

  5. Trim-out and finish

    After flooring, cabinets and tile, we set toilets, sinks, faucets, water heater and appliance connections.

  6. Final test and walkthrough

    We test every fixture, check for leaks and show you the main shutoff, cleanouts and water heater.

What happens at each stage of a typical build

Timing depends on your builder's schedule, weather and inspection availability. Rain delays concrete. Inspector backlogs delay drywall. A good plumber keeps those handoffs tight, so the framing crew, insulation crew and drywall crew are never waiting on plumbing.

Rough-in is a construction-stage service: piping before walls, floors and finished fixtures exist. Repairing an existing home is different work, covered in our water line repair and sewer line repair pages.

Plumbing Finish-Out

Finish-out, or trim-out, is when the plumbing becomes visible. After walls are painted, cabinets set and floors laid, we install fixtures and make final connections. It is the most visible work, and the easiest to damage if done carelessly around finished surfaces.

What trim-out includes

  • Toilets set on new wax or gasket seals, bolted level and tested
  • Sink faucets, supply stops and P-traps connected without kinks or leaks
  • Tub and shower trim over the valves set during rough-in
  • Water heater final connections, relief valve and drain piping
  • Dishwasher, ice-maker and refrigerator water lines, plus garbage disposals
  • Hose bibs, laundry boxes and any outdoor connections

Fixtures: who supplies what

Many builders supply fixtures. Some owner-builders buy their own. Either way, confirm model numbers before rough-in. The rough-in valve for a shower trim kit must match the trim brand, and island sinks, pot fillers and tankless heaters have specific supply and venting needs. Late fixture changes are the most common cause of avoidable cost and delay.

Tip: Keep box manuals and spec sheets. If a warranty claim comes up later, installation documentation helps.

Builder Coordination and Inspections

Plumbing inspections are a gate. If rough plumbing is not approved, insulation and drywall cannot proceed, and the whole schedule stalls. In San Antonio, the Development Services Department governs permits and inspections for residential work, and its published Residential Inspection Guide lists the order of required inspections: top-out plumbing, gas rough-in, mechanical, electrical rough-in and framing must all pass before the frame inspection moves forward.

Who pulls the permit and schedules inspections?

Plumbing permits are pulled by the licensed plumbing contractor. We manage that for our scope and schedule inspections as each phase is ready. Your general contractor still controls the overall build order, so we communicate with them directly and tell you when each inspection is booked.

How we work with builders

  • Plan review before the first trench is dug
  • Written scope and stage-by-stage payment terms
  • Photos of exposed rough-in before walls close
  • Clean job sites, capped stub-ups and protected finished surfaces

San Antonio Seasons and Your New Plumbing

Winter freezes. The February 2021 freeze showed how vulnerable exposed supply lines are in attics, exterior walls and outdoor bibs. In new construction we can route supply away from exterior walls where the design allows, insulate runs, and install freeze-protected hose bibs. Learn more about frozen pipe emergencies.

Summer drought. Extended dry spells make clay shrink and slabs shift. Quality bedding, sleeves and no stressed connections reduce risk of ground-movement damage.

Spring and fall rain. Heavy rain delays concrete, floods trenches and can contaminate open pipe. Cap every stub-up and keep ground-phase pipes clean before the pour.

Hard water year-round. The Edwards Aquifer supplies San Antonio, and its water is hard. It scales water heaters and fixtures. New homes are the best time to plan a water softener loop or filtration connection.

Holiday deadlines. Many families want to move in before the holidays. Start plumbing planning at least a few weeks before framing so inspection and fixture lead times do not become the bottleneck.

Questions to Ask Any New Construction Plumber

Whether you are a production builder or building your first home, these questions separate reliable plumbers from risky ones.

  • Who pulls the permit, and who books each inspection? The answer should be specific.
  • Can I see the written scope, including exclusions? Exclusions are where surprise charges hide.
  • How is payment staged? Draws should follow completed phases, not arrive as a large upfront cash demand.
  • What happens if we fail inspection? The plumber should fix it promptly at their own cost if the error is theirs.
  • Who is on site? Know whether the licensed plumber, a journeyman or a helper will do the work.
  • How do you protect stub-ups and finished surfaces?
  • Can you share photos of rough-in before cover?

Why New Construction Plumbing Is Not a DIY Job

Watching videos makes plumbing look simple. New construction is not like swapping a faucet. You are building a system that must meet code, pass inspection, and stay leak-free inside walls and under concrete for decades.

Don’t DIY this. Underground and in-wall plumbing needs a licensed plumber and city permits. Unpermitted work can void insurance coverage, fail inspection and complicate selling the home later.
  • Safety: Gas piping errors can cause leaks or carbon monoxide risks.
  • Liability: If you handle your own plumbing, any failure is yours.
  • Cost: A single wrong slope under the slab can mean breaking concrete to fix.
  • Warranty: Many builders and manufacturers require licensed installation.

You can still be involved. Choose fixtures, ask for photos, attend the walk-through and keep documents. Leave the pipes to us.

New Construction Plumbing FAQs: Straight Answers for San Antonio Homeowners

What plumbing work is involved in building a new house?

A complete system: underground drains and waste piping, water service line, in-wall supply and drain-waste-vent piping, gas piping where applicable, water heater, and all fixture connections. The work happens in three phases: ground, top-out rough-in, and trim-out.

When does rough-in plumbing occur?

Underground rough-in happens before the slab is poured. Top-out rough-in happens after framing and before insulation and drywall. Both must be inspected and approved before they are covered.

Who coordinates new construction plumbing inspections?

The licensed plumbing contractor pulls the permit and schedules plumbing inspections, while your general contractor controls the overall build sequence. We coordinate directly with your builder so inspections never hold up the job.

How much does new construction plumbing cost in San Antonio?

Cost depends on number of bathrooms, fixtures, slab layout, soil, materials and water heater type. Local rough-in often starts around $1,600 per bathroom, but whole-house totals vary. We give a written quote from your plans.

Can I choose my own fixtures?

Yes, but give us model numbers before rough-in, because valves, supply sizes and vent locations depend on them.

Do you work with owner-builders?

Yes. We explain each phase, handle permits and inspections for our scope, and keep communication simple.

What happens if the plumbing fails inspection?

We correct the issue and request re-inspection quickly. Failing early, before walls are closed, is far cheaper than finding problems after drywall.

Which pipe material is best for a new home?

PEX, copper and CPVC all have a place. The best choice depends on your builder’s spec, budget and water conditions. We will explain tradeoffs and install to your spec.

Can you plumb additions and ADUs?

Yes. Additions need tie-ins to existing lines, so we check capacity and condition before connecting new fixtures.
